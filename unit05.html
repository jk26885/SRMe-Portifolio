Unit 5

An Introduction to Security and Risk Standards in Industry and the Enterprise

This week focused on the role that industry and enterprise security standards play in security and risk management. The unit explored how standards are used to guide risk assessments, security design and organisational governance.

The objectives for this unit were to:

  • Explore industry specific security standards and directives.
  • Discuss how standards affect security and risk assessments.
  • Review a number of common industry and enterprise standards.
  • Identify and select the most appropriate security standard for a given situation.
  • Understand how standards can be incorporated into threat models.
  • Evaluate the role of standards in improving organisational security and resilience.

In this unit I examined several widely used frameworks including:

  • ISO/IEC 27001
  • NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)
  • COBIT
  • CIS Controls
  • PCI DSS
  • HITRUST
  • HIPAA

Each framework addresses specific organisational, operational or regulatory requirements. Selecting the most appropriate standard depends on factors such as industry sector, business objectives, regulatory obligations and organisational risk appetite.

Reflection

Unit 5 Reflection

This unit provided valuable insight into the relationship between risk management, security governance and industry standards. As an IT Systems Engineer working within large enterprise and cloud environments, I regularly encounter security frameworks such as ISO 27001, NIST, CIS Controls and Microsoft security baselines. This unit helped me better understand how these frameworks support organisational decision-making and risk management rather than serving purely as compliance checklists. I found the discussion on Enterprise Risk Management particularly relevant because it reflects the increasing need to manage technology risks alongside strategic business objectives. Modern organisations must address cybersecurity, cloud adoption, regulatory compliance and operational resilience simultaneously, making a structured ERM approach essential. The GDPR case study activity reinforced the importance of considering legal and regulatory obligations when designing security controls. It demonstrated that security decisions must balance technical requirements with privacy, governance and business considerations. The Cyber Essentials and NCSC guidance also highlighted that many successful cybersecurity programmes begin with strong foundational controls rather than complex technical solutions. Effective risk management often depends on implementing basic controls consistently across people, processes and technology.

Outcome

Unit Outcome

One of the learning outcomes for this unit was understanding how standards can be incorporated into threat models. I learned that threat models should not exist in isolation but should be aligned with organisational security requirements, risk management policies and compliance obligations. For example:

  • ISO 27001 provides security control requirements that can inform mitigation strategies.
  • NIST CSF helps identify and prioritise risks across the functions of Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover.
  • Cyber Essentials provides baseline controls that should be considered when evaluating attack scenarios.
  • GDPR requirements should be incorporated when modelling threats involving personal data.

Integrating standards into threat modelling ensures that identified threats are assessed within the broader context of organisational obligations, governance requirements and regulatory expectations. Overall, I achieved the learning outcomes by exploring key security standards, evaluating their suitability for different situations, understanding their impact on risk assessment and threat modelling, and recognising their role in supporting organisational resilience and governance.

Unit 10

Practical Applications and Issues in DR Implementations

This unit focused on the practical design and evaluation of disaster recovery (DR) solutions.

Unit Objectives:

    The objectives were to understand how Recovery Time Objectives (RTO) and Recovery Point Objectives (RPO) influence DR architecture, examine different standby and recovery solutions, and critically assess their limitations. The unit also considered DRaaS, vendor lock-in, resilience and network security.

My Objectives:

    My objective was to develop a better understanding of how business recovery requirements can be translated into an appropriate technical DR solution. I wanted to explore how different RTO and RPO requirements affect design choices and understand the trade-offs between availability, cost, complexity and security. I also wanted to examine whether cloud-based disaster recovery genuinely improves resilience or simply introduces different dependencies and risks.

Reflection

Unit 10 Reflection

This unit helped me move from defining recovery requirements to considering how they can actually be achieved through technical design. I learned that RTO and RPO should drive the selection of recovery technologies rather than being defined after a solution has already been chosen. A demanding RTO may require automated failover and continuously available standby resources, while a demanding RPO requires more frequent or continuous data replication. These capabilities can improve recovery performance but can also increase cost and complexity. The cloud-based DR approaches examined by Alhazmi and Malaiya (2013) helped me consider how cloud services can provide alternative recovery options. However, I recognised that using cloud or DRaaS does not automatically guarantee resilience. Recovery remains dependent on factors such as connectivity, configuration, security and the availability of the provider. The readings also made me consider the longer-term implications of vendor dependency. Kumar (2024) highlighted vendor lock-in as an important consideration, reinforcing my understanding that selecting a recovery platform is not simply a technical decision. Organisations also need to consider portability and how easily services and data could be moved if their requirements or provider circumstances change. Mission Thread Analysis also strengthened my understanding of resilience by encouraging consideration of dependencies across systems and services. This showed me that recovering an individual server or application does not necessarily restore the complete business capability if supporting networks, identity services, data sources or other dependencies remain unavailable. Overall, this unit reinforced the importance of critically evaluating DR designs rather than assuming that redundancy, cloud services or replication automatically provide resilience. A successful DR solution must meet business-defined recovery objectives while also considering security, dependencies, cost and the risks introduced by the recovery architecture itself.

Outcome

Unit Outcome

Having participated in DR exercises at work, I initially approached this unit with practical experience of recovery testing. However, the unit encouraged me to look beyond the successful restoration of systems and consider what actually constitutes a successful DR exercise. I now recognise that recovery should be evaluated against defined RTO and RPO requirements, as well as the availability of dependent services such as networks, identity services and supporting infrastructure. This has made me more conscious of the assumptions that should be challenged during future DR exercises and the importance of using test outcomes to improve resilience rather than treating the exercise simply as evidence that a recovery procedure works.

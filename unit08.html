Unit 8

Implementing Quantitative Risk Models

Unit Objectives:

  • Explore how QRM can be applied to real world problems.
  • Examine case studies of how QRM techniques are used.
  • Select an appropriate modelling technique for a given situation.

My objectives:

My objective was to build on the theoretical knowledge gained in Unit 7 by developing a more practical understanding of quantitative risk modelling. I wanted to learn how to translate real-world uncertainty into a model, choose appropriate inputs and assumptions, and interpret the results in a way that could support risk-informed decision-making. I was particularly interested in understanding when Monte Carlo simulation or Bayesian analysis would provide meaningful insight rather than simply producing numerical results.

Unit 8

Tasks Done

During this unit, I examined how Monte Carlo simulation and Bayes Theorem can be applied to practical risk scenarios, including project management and safety-related problems. I considered how uncertain variables can be represented through probability distributions and how repeated simulation can produce a range of possible outcomes. I also explored how Bayesian reasoning can incorporate new evidence into an existing risk assessment. Through the case studies and modelling activities, I considered how the choice of technique, quality of available evidence, assumptions and dependencies between variables can influence model outputs.

Reflection

Unit 8 Reflection

This unit helped me progress from understanding quantitative risk modelling in Unit 7 to considering how I would actually construct and evaluate a model. One of my main realisations was that running a simulation is often easier than deciding what should go into it. The choice of variables, probability distributions and assumptions can have a significant influence on the result. The Monte Carlo material was particularly useful because it changed how I think about uncertain risk outcomes. Rather than relying on one expected value, simulation allows uncertainty to be represented through a range of possible outcomes (Eckstein and Riedmueller, 2002). However, I also became more critical of the apparent precision produced by quantitative models. A large number of simulations cannot compensate for poorly justified inputs. This reminded me that the credibility of a model depends as much on its assumptions and data as on the mathematical technique itself. The recommended readings also helped me see QRM from a cybersecurity perspective. The combination of attack trees and Markov modelling presented by Aijaz and Nazir (2024) demonstrated that security threats can be modelled as evolving processes rather than isolated events. Similarly, Zografopoulos et al. (2021) highlighted the complexity of assessing cybersecurity risk in interconnected cyber-physical environments. This was particularly relevant to my own professional interest in cybersecurity because dependencies between systems mean that risks may not always behave independently.

Outcome

Unit Outcome

My most important learning from the unit is therefore that model selection should follow the problem, rather than forcing a problem into a preferred modelling technique. Monte Carlo simulation may be appropriate when several uncertain variables affect an outcome, whereas Bayesian approaches may be more valuable when probabilities need to change as new evidence becomes available. I developed greater confidence in moving from a real-world risk problem towards an appropriate quantitative model. I can now better consider what variables need to be represented, how uncertainty should be incorporated and whether the selected modelling technique is appropriate for the available evidence. Overall, the unit strengthened my understanding that effective quantitative risk modelling requires both technical modelling skills and critical judgement. The value of the model is ultimately determined not by how sophisticated it appears, but by how well it represents the real-world risk and supports an informed decision.

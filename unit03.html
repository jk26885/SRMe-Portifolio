Unit 3

Introduction to Threat Modelling and Management

In Unit 3, I explored threat modelling approaches including STRIDE, DREAD, Attack Trees and hybrid models such as the Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis (PASTA), alongside threat modelling guides and cookbooks. The aim was to understand how organisations choose a framework based on their security requirements, system characteristics and risk management objectives. I also contacted other students to begin work on the group assignment.

Artefact

Data, Technology, Fairness and Accountability Discussion

Activity: Initial discussion post and peer feedback

In my initial post, I examined how digital technologies can support human rights investigations while also creating risks of bias, surveillance, misinterpretation and weakened accountability. Drawing on Hancock et al. (2024), I argued that digital evidence such as satellite imagery and AIS data cannot be interpreted effectively without local and contextual knowledge, and that participatory design, governance and independent oversight are needed to represent affected communities.

Two peer responses extended my thinking. The first introduced practical safeguards, particularly Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) verification and privacy-preserving approaches. The second challenged me to consider how organisations can ensure community participation genuinely influences decisions rather than becoming a procedural exercise.

Evidence of learning

My initial focus was on the ethical and governance risks of digital investigation. Peer feedback added practical safeguards and the quality of stakeholder participation, leading to a more balanced view: technology alone cannot ensure fairness or accountability, so technical controls must be combined with human judgement, contextual knowledge, governance and meaningful community involvement.

Reflection

Unit 3 Reflection

This unit introduced threat modelling as a structured, proactive way to identify security risks before they materialise. A key learning point was the distinction between vulnerabilities, which are weaknesses that may be exploited, and threats, which are the actors, events or circumstances capable of exploiting them. Vulnerabilities therefore need to be judged within their specific threat and organisational context.

The methodologies showed that no single approach suits every situation: STRIDE identifies technical threats, Attack Trees map possible attack paths, and PASTA links vulnerabilities to business objectives. Selection should reflect the system, organisational objectives and available resources. Combining techniques felt particularly relevant to my professional experience with hybrid infrastructure, where cloud services, networks, applications and third-party dependencies all interact.

Most importantly, the unit reinforced the value of threat analysis early in the system lifecycle. Identifying weaknesses during design supports security by design and strengthened my ability to analyse systems from an attacker’s perspective.

Outcome

Unit Outcome

I can now distinguish clearly between threats and vulnerabilities, select and combine threat modelling techniques to suit different contexts, and explain how integrating them into system design supports secure, resilient and well-governed technology environments.

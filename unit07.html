Unit 7

An Introduction to the Concepts of Quantitative Risk Modelling

This unit introduced quantitative risk modelling (QRM) and explored techniques including Monte Carlo simulation, Bayesian methods, TOPSIS, AHP and ANP. The unit focused on selecting appropriate quantitative techniques for real-world risk problems and critically evaluating their strengths, limitations and potential antipatterns.

My objectives:

My objective was to move beyond viewing risk mainly through qualitative categories such as high, medium and low and develop a better understanding of how uncertainty can be expressed numerically. I wanted to understand not only how quantitative models work, but also how to determine whether the results they produce are reliable enough to support real-world decisions.

Unit 7

Tasks Done

During the unit, I explored different quantitative risk modelling approaches and considered the types of problems for which each is best suited. I examined Monte Carlo simulation for modelling uncertainty through repeated simulations and Bayesian approaches for updating risk estimates as new evidence becomes available. I also reviewed AHP, ANP and TOPSIS as approaches for evaluating alternatives where decisions involve multiple criteria.

Reflection

Unit 7 Reflection

Before this unit, I tended to see quantitative risk assessment as inherently more objective than qualitative assessment because it produces numerical results. The unit challenged this assumption. I realised that adding probabilities and mathematical models does not automatically make an assessment more accurate. The quality of the result remains heavily dependent on the quality of the data, the assumptions made and whether the selected model genuinely represents the problem. Monte Carlo simulation particularly changed how I think about uncertainty. Rather than expecting a single estimate of what will happen, I learned to consider a range of possible outcomes and their associated probabilities. At the same time, I recognised the danger of treating simulation results as facts. If the underlying probability distributions or assumptions are weak, the resulting model may give a false impression of precision. Bayesian modelling also made me reconsider how risk assessments should respond to new information. I found the idea of updating an existing probability when additional evidence becomes available particularly useful because risk is rarely static. However, I also recognised that the choice of prior assumptions can influence the result, making transparency about assumptions essential. The comparison with AHP, ANP and TOPSIS further demonstrated that there is no universally superior quantitative technique. The appropriate method depends on the question being asked, the available evidence and the nature of the decision. This was perhaps my most important learning from the unit: the ability to justify why a model is appropriate is as important as being able to perform the modelling itself.

Outcome

Unit Outcome

This unit has changed how I approach quantitative risk analysis. I now look beyond the numerical output and question where the data came from, what assumptions were made, how uncertainty was represented and whether dependencies between risks have been considered. I feel more confident in selecting between probabilistic and multi-criteria techniques based on the nature of a risk problem. More importantly, I have developed a more critical approach to interpreting their results. In future risk assessments, I would use quantitative models as decision-support tools rather than definitive predictions, combining their results with professional judgement and wider organisational context. This unit therefore strengthened both my technical understanding of quantitative risk modelling and my ability to critically evaluate whether a model provides a credible basis for risk-informed decision-making. What I changed: I shifted the emphasis from describing the techniques to showing your learning journey, particularly your initial assumptions, what challenged them, what you now understand differently, and how this will influence your future approach to risk assessment.

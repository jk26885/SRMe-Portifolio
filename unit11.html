Unit 11

Future Trends in Security and Risk Management

This unit explored current and emerging trends in Security and Risk Management (SRM), including developments in economic theory, behavioural approaches, artificial intelligence (AI) and automation.

Unit Objectives:

    The unit aimed to critically evaluate the advantages and limitations of these approaches, consider their potential influence on future research and practice, and identify which trends are likely to have the greatest impact on the future of risk management.

My objectives:

My objective was to broaden my understanding of risk management beyond the traditional frameworks and techniques covered earlier in the module. I wanted to explore how economic models, behavioural factors, AI and automation could support risk-based decision-making. I was particularly interested in understanding whether emerging technologies can improve risk assessment and cybersecurity decisions while still accounting for uncertainty, organisational context and human judgement.

Tasks Done

  • Reviewed emerging trends and approaches in security and risk management.
  • Explored Real Options Theory and its application to risk and investment decisions.
  • Considered quantitative approaches such as Monte Carlo simulation and Bayesian methods in relation to uncertainty.
  • Examined behavioural operations and the influence of cognitive bias and human judgement on risk decisions.
  • Reviewed developments in machine learning and autonomous cyber defence.
  • Considered emerging risks associated with complex supply chains and technology dependencies.
  • Reflected on the importance of selecting risk frameworks that are appropriate to organisational needs rather than applying a single approach universally.
  • Considered the advantages and limitations of increasing automation within security and risk-management processes.
Reflection

Unit 11 Reflection

This unit broadened my understanding of the range of approaches that can contribute to security and risk-management decisions. Earlier in the module, I had considered techniques such as quantitative risk analysis, Monte Carlo simulation, vulnerability scoring and business continuity planning. Unit 11 helped me recognise that risk management continues to evolve and increasingly incorporates ideas from economics, behavioural science and artificial intelligence. A particularly important learning point for me was that risk models should support rather than replace informed decision-making. Aven and Thekdi (2025) reinforced the importance of considering uncertainty and the strength of available knowledge when assessing risk. This relates closely to my earlier work with vulnerability frameworks such as CVSS, EPSS and SSVC, where I recognised that numerical measures can be valuable but cannot independently capture an organisation's complete risk context. I also found the behavioural perspective particularly relevant. Fahimnia et al. (2019) demonstrate the importance of human behaviour in operational and supply-chain decision-making. This reminded me that even sophisticated risk models are ultimately used within organisations where decisions may be influenced by experience, judgement and cognitive bias. Understanding these influences is therefore as important as improving the technical accuracy of the models themselves. Of the trends examined, I believe AI and automation will have one of the greatest influences on the future of security and risk management. Machine learning can support the analysis of large volumes of security information and contribute to increasingly autonomous cyber-defence capabilities (Ridley, McCloskey and Mountain, 2018). From my own professional perspective in IT systems and security, this is particularly relevant because the volume and speed of security information can make purely manual analysis increasingly difficult. However, this unit also made me more cautious about assuming that automation necessarily results in better risk decisions. Automated outputs remain dependent on the quality of data, underlying assumptions and the context in which they are interpreted. I therefore see the future of SRM not as replacing human decision-making with AI, but as combining automation and quantitative analysis with professional judgement, organisational knowledge and effective governance.

Outcome

Unit Outcome

I have developed a broader understanding of emerging trends in security and risk management and how approaches from economics, behavioural science and artificial intelligence can contribute to risk-based decision-making. The most significant outcome for me is recognising that there is unlikely to be a single model or technology that defines the future of risk management. Different techniques address different aspects of uncertainty and decision-making. Economic and quantitative models can support structured analysis, behavioural approaches can help explain how people make risk decisions, while AI and automation can increase the speed and scale at which information is analysed. I consider AI and automation to be among the most influential future trends, particularly in cybersecurity. However, their effectiveness will depend on how successfully organisations combine them with human expertise, contextual understanding and appropriate risk governance. Overall, this unit has strengthened my view of risk management as an evolving discipline in which technology enhances decision-making but does not remove the need for critical judgement.

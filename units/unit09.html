Unit 9

Risk, Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

Unit Objectives:

    The objectives were to understand how BC and DR plans are developed, examine the importance of Business Impact Assessments (BIA), Recovery Time Objectives (RTO) and Recovery Point Objectives (RPO), and consider emerging developments in Information Risk Management. The unit also aimed to develop the ability to select appropriate recovery solutions based on organisational requirements.

My Objectives:

    My objective was to strengthen my understanding of how risk assessment translates into practical resilience and recovery decisions. In particular, I wanted to understand how a BIA can be used to identify critical services and establish appropriate RTO and RPO requirements. I also wanted to consider how technical recovery solutions, including redundancy, replication and disaster recovery services, can be aligned with business requirements rather than selected purely from a technical perspective.

Tasks Done

  • Reviewed the principles of business continuity and disaster recovery.
  • Examined the relationship between BIA, RTO, RPO and recovery strategies.
  • Reviewed Andrade et al. (2017) to understand the relationship between disaster-recovery architecture and service availability.
  • Considered risk assessment and treatment from the broader perspective presented by Popov, Lyon and Hollcroft (2022).
  • Participated in discussions about vulnerability management and compared CVSS, EPSS and SSVC as approaches to vulnerability prioritisation.
  • Reflected on how vulnerability assessment, risk treatment, business continuity and disaster recovery contribute to organisational resilience.
Reflection

Unit 9 Reflection

This unit helped me recognise that disaster recovery should not begin with choosing a technical solution. The starting point should be understanding what the organisation needs to protect and the consequences if a critical service becomes unavailable. A BIA therefore provides an important connection between business requirements and technical recovery planning. RTO defines how quickly a service should be restored, while RPO establishes the acceptable level of data loss. These objectives can then guide decisions about backup, replication, redundancy and failover. The reading by Andrade et al. (2017) reinforced my understanding that availability and disaster-recovery solutions can be evaluated systematically rather than simply assumed to provide resilience. This made me consider recovery architecture as part of the wider risk-management process, where the level of protection should reflect the importance of the service and the organisation's recovery requirements. I also connected this learning with the vulnerability-management discussion. My examination of CVSS, EPSS and SSVC demonstrated that technical severity alone does not determine organisational risk. A severe vulnerability affecting a non-critical or isolated asset may present a different business risk from a vulnerability affecting a service essential to operations. Similarly, BC/DR decisions require an understanding of business impact and context rather than relying exclusively on technical measures. This unit therefore changed my perspective from viewing disaster recovery primarily as an IT function to seeing it as part of broader organisational resilience. Effective recovery requires technical capability, but that capability must be driven by business priorities, risk assessment and clearly defined recovery objectives.

Outcome

Unit Outcome

In this unit, I developed a clearer understanding of BC, DR, BIA, RTO and RPO and how these concepts work together. I can now relate recovery requirements to suitable technical and organisational controls and recognise the importance of balancing recovery capability against business impact and risk. More importantly, I can see a clearer connection between the different risk-management approaches covered in the module. Frameworks such as CVSS, EPSS and SSVC help identify and prioritise vulnerabilities, while risk assessment and BIA establish their potential organisational consequences. Business continuity and disaster recovery then provide mechanisms for maintaining or restoring critical operations when disruption cannot be prevented. This has strengthened my understanding of information risk management as a continuous process involving identification, assessment, treatment, resilience and recovery.
